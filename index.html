The engineering design process

Engineering isn't about getting it right the first time. It's a way of working that gets you to something that works, one test at a time.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Describe the steps of the engineering design process and why it loops.
  • Tell criteria from constraints, and compare ideas with a weighted decision matrix.
  • Treat a failed test as information and record your work in an engineering notebook.

What engineers do

Engineers use science and math to solve problems for people. A bridge that carries traffic, a water filter, a phone app, a prosthetic hand: each one started with someone who had a problem and an engineer who asked, "How could we fix that?"

Engineers almost never get to design whatever they like. They work within limits: a budget, a deadline, the materials available, safety rules, the space something has to fit in. Solving a problem well inside those limits is the heart of the job.

The design process is a loop

Different textbooks draw it with slightly different boxes, but the engineering design process as it's commonly taught looks like this:

  1. Define the problem. Who is it for? What must the design do? What limits do you have to work within?
  2. Research. How have other people solved similar problems? What do you need to know or measure?
  3. Brainstorm. Come up with many ideas, including odd ones. Don't judge yet.
  4. Choose. Compare your best ideas against what the design needs to do, and pick one.
  5. Build a prototype. Make a working version, often a rough one, so you can test it.
  6. Test. Check the prototype against your requirements and write down what happens.
  7. Improve. Fix what failed, then test again.
  8. Communicate. Share the design, your results and your reasons so others can use or build on it.

It's a loop, not a straight line. Testing usually sends you back to improve, and sometimes all the way back to redefine the problem. Each pass through the loop is called an iteration.

Criteria, constraints and trade-offs

Defining the problem means writing down two kinds of things:

  • Criteria are what a good design should do, the qualities you use to judge and compare ideas: how stable, how easy to use, how good it looks.
  • Constraints are limits the design must stay within, no matter how good it is otherwise: cost, time, materials, size and safety.

An idea that scores well on every criterion but breaks a constraint is out. A $50 design doesn't count if your budget is $5.

You rarely get the best of everything. A sturdier design may cost more or take longer to build. Giving up some of one thing to get more of another is a trade-off, and engineers make them all the time.

A decision matrix helps you make trade-offs on purpose. List your criteria, give each a weight for how much it matters, and score each idea (say, 1 to 5) on every criterion. Multiply each score by its weight and add them up. The highest weighted total is a strong candidate, not an automatic winner, but it shows clearly why you chose what you did.

Worked example

Ravi watches video lessons on his phone while taking notes, and his phone keeps sliding off the stack of books he leans it on. He decides to design a phone stand for his desk.

Define. His criteria: it's stable and doesn't tip when he taps the screen, holds the phone at a comfortable viewing angle, is easy to build, leaves room for the charger cable, and is cheap. His constraints: no more than $5 to spend, built in one weekend from materials at home, hand tools only with safety glasses and an adult nearby, and no sharp edges or splinters.

Brainstorm and choose. He sketches eight ideas and picks three to score: (A) a folded cardboard triangle, (B) two large binder clips clipped together, and (C) a scrap-wood base with an angled backrest and a small front lip. Stability matters most to him, so he gives it a weight of 3.

Prototype and test. Before building, Ravi writes three tests: the charger plugs in while the phone sits in the stand, he can read the screen comfortably from his chair, and the stand doesn't tip or slide when he taps the top of the screen 10 times. On the retest the stand tips 0 times in 10 taps, and he reruns the other two tests to make sure the fix didn't break anything. Both still pass.

Failure is information

In engineering, a failed test isn't a bad grade. It's data. It tells you exactly where your design and the real world disagree, which is the most useful thing you can learn early, while changes are cheap.

That's why engineers build prototypes, often rough ones from cardboard, tape or scrap material. It's far better to find out a stand tips over when it's made of scrap wood than after you've made a hundred of them.

When something fails, ask three questions: What happened? Why did it happen? What one change would fix it? Change one thing at a time, then test again. If you change five things at once and it works, you won't know which change mattered.

Common mistake

Falling in love with your first idea and jumping straight to building. If you skip defining the problem and brainstorming, you have nothing to judge your idea against, and you'll never know whether a better one was sitting right next to it. Write the criteria and constraints down first, and come up with at least three ideas before choosing.

Keep an engineering notebook

An engineering notebook is a dated record of your whole design: the problem statement, criteria and constraints, sketches of every idea (even rejected ones), your decision matrix, measurements, test results and what you changed after each test.

Write in it as you go, not afterward. Date each page, label your sketches, and never tear out pages. Cross mistakes out with a single line so they stay readable. A failed idea you recorded can save you, or a teammate, from trying it again.

The notebook also makes the last step, communicating, much easier. When someone asks "Why did you build it that way?", the answer is already written down.

Key idea

Define the problem before you solve it, compare several ideas against your criteria and constraints, and treat every test, especially a failed one, as information that makes the next version better.

Sketch three ideas and score them in a small decision matrix. Build the winner, then test it: does it hold the book for one minute without collapsing? Record what failed, change one thing, and test again. Put it all in a notebook page with today's date.

Engineering fields and careers

"Engineer" isn't one job. It's a whole family of jobs, and almost anything you care about has a field of engineering behind it.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Describe what the major engineering fields work on, with an everyday example of each.
  • Explain how engineers, technicians and technologists differ, and how someone becomes a licensed engineer.
  • Match your own interests to fields and plan ways to explore them in high school.

The major fields

Every field of engineering uses the design process from Lesson 1. What changes is what they design. Here are the big ones, each with something you've probably used this week:

  • Mechanical engineers design things that move or use energy and heat: engines, pumps, robots, refrigerators. Example: the gears and brakes on a bicycle.
  • Electrical engineers work with electricity, from power lines to tiny circuits. Example: the charger that safely turns power from the wall into what your phone can use.
  • Civil engineers design roads, bridges, tunnels, dams and water systems. Structural engineering is a specialty within civil that makes sure buildings and bridges carry their loads safely. Example: the road you take to school.
  • Chemical engineers turn raw materials into useful products by designing large-scale chemical processes. Example: the factory process that makes toothpaste by the ton.
  • Computer engineers design computer hardware and the place where hardware meets software, like chips and circuit boards. Software engineers design, build and test programs. Example: the apps on your phone and the chip that runs them.
  • Biomedical engineers combine engineering with biology and medicine. Example: a hearing aid, or a prosthetic (artificial) leg.
  • Aerospace engineers design aircraft, spacecraft and satellites. Example: the wings of a passenger airplane.
  • Environmental engineers protect people and nature by dealing with water, air, soil and waste. Example: the treatment plant that makes your tap water safe to drink.
  • Industrial engineers make systems and processes work better, faster and more safely. Example: how a warehouse picks, packs and ships orders without wasted steps.

Big projects need many fields

Real projects rarely belong to one field. Think about a hospital. Civil and structural engineers design the building and its foundation. Mechanical engineers handle heating, cooling and fresh air. Electrical engineers plan the power, including backup power for when the grid goes down. Biomedical engineers work on the medical equipment, software engineers build the patient-record systems, and industrial engineers plan how patients and staff move through it all.

A phone needs electrical and computer engineers for the circuits, software engineers for the apps, mechanical engineers for the case and for keeping it cool, chemical engineers for battery materials, and industrial engineers to build millions of them. A bridge needs structural engineers, plus environmental engineers protecting the river underneath.

That's why teamwork matters: you'll often work with people who know things you don't.

Common mistake

Choosing, or ruling out, a field based only on its name. Mechanical engineering isn't about fixing cars, and computer engineering isn't about repairing laptops; those are different jobs. And biology isn't only for doctors: biomedical, chemical and environmental engineers all use it. Read what a field actually works on before deciding it's not for you.

Engineers, technicians and technologists

Engineering teams include more than engineers. In general:

  • Engineers focus on designing and analyzing: deciding what to build and using math and science to predict whether it will work.
  • Engineering technologists usually study engineering technology, which is more hands-on and applied. They often put designs into practice, improve processes and run tests.
  • Engineering technicians often train through a shorter program, such as a two-year associate degree. They build, test, measure, repair and maintain equipment, and collect the data engineers rely on.

Titles and duties vary by employer, and every role matters: a design is only as good as the people who build, test and maintain it.

Degrees and licensure

Most engineers earn a four-year bachelor's degree in engineering. Whatever the field, you can expect math through calculus and beyond, physics, chemistry, design projects, programming and lab courses where you test real things. Many programs share a common set of first-year courses before you specialize.

In the US, look for programs accredited by ABET, an organization that reviews engineering programs to check that they meet quality standards. It matters later if you want a license.

A Professional Engineer (PE) license lets an engineer take legal responsibility for work that affects the public, such as signing off on plans. Each state sets its own rules, but the path generally looks like this:

  1. Earn a degree from an accredited engineering program.
  2. Pass the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam, often near or just after graduation.
  3. Gain several years of work experience under licensed engineers.
  4. Pass the Principles and Practice of Engineering (PE) exam in your field.

Licensure is especially common in civil engineering and related fields, where engineers approve public projects like roads and water systems. Many engineers in other fields, especially those designing products inside companies, work without one.

Skills for every field, and how to start now

Some skills matter no matter which field you pick: problem solving, math, communication, teamwork and writing. Engineers write reports, emails, test results and instructions all the time, and a great design that no one understands never gets built.

You don't have to wait for college to explore. In high school you can:

  • Take the most challenging math and science classes you can handle, plus any design, programming or shop classes offered.
  • Join a robotics or engineering club, or start one with a teacher's help.
  • Enter a science fair with an engineering project.
  • Look for summer engineering programs; many offer financial aid.
  • Ask about job shadowing: spending a day following an engineer at work.
  • Do an informational interview: a short conversation where you ask someone about their job.
  • Build things at home, safely. Wear safety glasses, have an adult supervise any tools, and stick to small batteries for anything electrical. Never experiment with wall outlets or household wiring.

Measurement, units and estimation

Engineers can't build what they can't measure. A good measurement has a number, a unit, and an honest sense of how close it is to the truth.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Use SI units and prefixes, and convert between units step by step.
  • Tell precision from accuracy, and calculate percent error.
  • Make a quick estimate and sanity-check an answer before you trust it.

Why units matter

If someone tells you a board is "3 long", you can't cut it. Three what? Three millimeters, three feet and three meters are completely different boards. A number without a unit is meaningless. Always write the unit, even in the middle of a calculation.

Mixing units is one of the most common ways real engineering goes wrong. When one team works in inches and pounds and another assumes centimeters and kilograms, every number can look reasonable while the parts don't fit, a load is calculated wrong, or a system behaves in a way nobody planned. Mistakes like this have ruined expensive projects. The fix is simple: agree on units up front, label everything, and convert carefully when you have to.

SI units and prefixes

Scientists and engineers around the world use the International System of Units (SI), the modern metric system. It has seven base units. These five are the ones you'll use most:

  • Meter (m) for length.
  • Kilogram (kg) for mass.
  • Second (s) for time.
  • Ampere (A) for electric current, which you'll meet in Lesson 5.
  • Kelvin (K) for temperature. A change of 1 K is the same size as a change of 1 °C, and 0 °C is exactly 273.15 K, so room temperature of about 20 °C is about 293 K.

(The other two are the mole, for amount of substance, and the candela, for brightness of light.)

Prefixes scale a unit up or down by powers of ten, and these conversions are exact:

  • milli- (m) means one thousandth: 1 mm = 0.001 m, so 1 m = 1,000 mm.
  • centi- (c) means one hundredth: 1 cm = 0.01 m, so 1 m = 100 cm.
  • kilo- (k) means one thousand: 1 km = 1,000 m, and 1 kg = 1,000 g.
  • mega- (M) means one million: 1 megawatt (MW) = 1,000,000 watts.

In the US you'll also see US customary units such as inches, feet, pounds and miles. Engineers here often use both, so conversion is a core skill.

Converting units

A conversion factor is a fraction whose top and bottom are equal amounts in different units, like 2.54 cm over 1 in. Because the top equals the bottom, multiplying by it changes the units without changing the actual size. Some useful ones:

  • 1 inch = 2.54 cm (exact, by definition)
  • 1 kg ≈ 2.205 lb
  • 1 mile ≈ 1.609 km

To convert, write the value with its unit, then multiply by the factor arranged so the unit you don't want cancels:

  1. Start with what you have: 150 lb.
  2. Pick the factor with pounds on the bottom so they cancel: 1 kg / 2.205 lb.
  3. Multiply: 150 lb × (1 kg / 2.205 lb) = 150 ÷ 2.205 kg ≈ 68.03 kg, or about 68 kg.

The same method turns a 12-inch ruler into centimeters: 12 in × (2.54 cm / 1 in) = 30.48 cm. If the units don't cancel the way you want, the factor is upside down.

Precision, accuracy and percent error

These two words sound alike but mean different things. Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other. Picture darts: a tight cluster far from the bullseye is precise but not accurate. Darts scattered evenly around the bullseye are accurate on average but not precise. Good measurements are both.

Every measurement has some error, the difference between what you measured and the true, or accepted, value. Error isn't a blunder; it comes from the tool's limits, reading a scale at an angle, or the object itself. Because no part can be made perfectly either, drawings give a tolerance: a part marked "10.0 mm ± 0.1 mm" is acceptable anywhere from 9.9 mm to 10.1 mm. A part measuring 10.08 mm passes; one measuring 9.85 mm is rejected.

To compare errors fairly, engineers use percent error:

percent error = |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100

The bars mean "absolute value", so percent error is never negative. Being off by 1 cm on a 10 cm pencil is a 10% error; being off by 1 cm on a 100 m track is only 0.01%. Try the calculator below with your own measurements.

Common mistake

Copying every digit your calculator shows. Tomás's 124.5 cm converts to 49.015748... inches, but his tape only reads to the nearest millimeter, so writing all those digits claims precision he doesn't have. Round your answer to about as many digits as your measurement actually supports: 49.0 in.

Estimation and sanity checks

Engineers often need a rough answer fast, before any careful calculation. A back-of-the-envelope or Fermi estimate breaks a big question into pieces you can guess sensibly, then multiplies them out using round numbers. The goal is the right size, not the exact value.

How many times does a car tire turn in one mile? A typical car tire is about 0.65 m across, so its circumference is about π × 0.65 m ≈ 2 m. A mile is about 1,600 m. So 1,600 m ÷ 2 m per turn ≈ 800 turns. A careful calculation gives about 790, so the estimate is close.

Estimates also help you sanity-check answers. Before you trust a result, ask:

  • Does it make sense? A desk 1.2 km long or a backpack weighing 3 g means something went wrong.
  • Do the units work out? If you're finding a length and your answer comes out in kilograms, look for the mistake.
  • Is it close to your estimate? If your careful answer is 100 times your rough one, check for a misplaced prefix like milli- or kilo-.

Key idea

Every number needs a unit. Convert with factors that cancel, report how close you are with percent error, and check every answer against a quick estimate before you trust it.

Forces, structures and materials

Every bridge, bookshelf and bike frame is quietly pushing and pulling against the forces on it. Engineers decide how it will win.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Measure force in newtons and work out an object's weight from its mass.
  • Spot tension and compression in structures, and explain why triangles and trusses are so common.
  • Use the lever balance rule to find the effort needed and the mechanical advantage.

Force and the newton

A force is a push or a pull. In SI units it's measured in newtons (N). One newton is the force that speeds up a 1 kg mass by 1 meter per second, every second. A 100 g apple in your hand pushes down with about 1 N.

Weight is the pull of gravity on a mass. On Earth, that's about 9.8 N for every kilogram:

weight (N) ≈ mass (kg) × 9.8 m/s²

A 1 kg bag of rice weighs about 9.8 N. A 60 kg person weighs about 588 N. In US customary units, force is measured in pounds-force (lbf), and 1 lbf is about 4.45 N.

Common mistake

Mixing up mass and weight. Mass, in kilograms, is how much stuff an object has. Weight, in newtons, is the force of gravity on it. If a problem gives you 40 kg and needs a force, multiply by 9.8 first, or your answer will be almost ten times too small.

Loads, tension and compression

The forces a structure carries are called loads. There are two broad kinds:

  • Dead load is the weight of the structure itself and anything permanently attached, like beams, floors and walls. It's always there.
  • Live load is anything that comes and goes: people, furniture, cars on a bridge, snow on a roof. Designers plan for the heavy cases.

Inside a structure, loads become two basic kinds of force. Tension pulls a part apart, like a rope in a tug-of-war. Compression squeezes it, like a column holding up a porch roof. Stress is force spread over an area, so the same force on a thinner part means more stress.

Triangles, trusses and beams

Pin four sticks into a square and it folds into a diamond with almost no push. Pin three into a triangle and it won't budge: with three side lengths fixed, a triangle can only have one shape, so it can't deform unless a side stretches or squashes. That's why triangles are called rigid.

A truss is a frame made of connected triangles. You'll see them in bridges, roof frames and cranes. Each member is mostly in tension or compression, so a truss carries big loads with little material.

A beam carries loads across a gap, like a shelf or a plank over a stream. Picture a beam resting on a support at each end with a load on the middle. As it sags, the top gets squeezed (compression) while the bottom gets stretched (tension). Bend a rubber eraser and you can see it happen.

Materials and the factor of safety

Choosing a material means trading off several properties:

  • Strength: how much stress it can take before it breaks or permanently bends.
  • Stiffness: how much it resists bending or stretching. Glass is stiff, yet it shatters easily.
  • Weight: lighter matters for anything that moves or has to be carried.
  • Cost, and how easy it is to cut and join.

Materials can behave differently in tension and compression. Steel is strong in tension. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension: a plain concrete beam cracks along its stretched bottom. So builders cast steel bars into the concrete where tension will be. That's reinforced concrete: each material does what it's good at.

Engineers never design a part to hold only the load they expect. They build in a factor of safety: the load that would make it fail divided by the expected load. A shelf expected to hold 200 N that would only fail at 600 N has a factor of safety of 3. The margin covers surprises: heavier loads, flaws in the material, wear over time. For real structures, building codes set how big it must be.

Levers and mechanical advantage

A lever is a stiff bar that turns around a pivot point called the fulcrum. Seesaws and pry bars are levers. The force you apply is the effort; what you're moving is the load.

A force's turning effect is called its moment (or torque):

moment = force × distance from the fulcrum

In newtons and meters, moment is in newton-meters (N·m). A lever balances when the moments on each side are equal:

load × load distance = effort × effort distance

So the effort needed is load × load distance ÷ effort distance. The mechanical advantage, how many times the lever multiplies your force, is effort distance ÷ load distance for an ideal lever with no friction. The trade-off: your end moves farther than the load does, by the same ratio. You get an easier push, over a longer distance.

Electricity and circuits

Every phone, flashlight and robot runs on the same few ideas: a push, a flow and something that resists it. Learn how they fit and you can predict what a circuit will do before you build it.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain current, voltage and resistance, and tell a closed circuit from an open or short one.
  • Use Ohm's law and P = V × I to find current and power in a simple circuit.
  • Compare series and parallel circuits and work out their total resistance.

Safety first

Everything in this lesson uses small batteries (about 1.5 V to 9 V) and low-power parts, like those in a classroom electronics set. At that level, the circuits here are safe to build with care.

  • Never experiment with wall outlets, household wiring, extension cords or anything that plugs into the wall. Household electricity can kill.
  • Never experiment with large batteries, such as car batteries or the packs in power tools, e-bikes or scooters. They can deliver enough current to cause burns and fires.
  • Work with an adult or teacher supervising, and wear safety glasses when you cut or strip wire.
  • If a part, wire or battery gets hot, smells or swells, disconnect it and stop.

Charge, current, voltage and resistance

Atoms contain tiny particles with electric charge. Electrons carry negative charge, and in metals some are free to move. Charge is measured in coulombs (C).

  • Current is how much charge flows past a point each second, measured in amperes (A), or amps. 1 A means 1 coulomb per second. Small circuits often use milliamps (mA): 1 A = 1,000 mA.
  • Voltage is the push that drives charge around a circuit, measured in volts (V). A battery provides a voltage between its two terminals.
  • Resistance is how strongly something opposes current, measured in ohms (Ω).

A handy picture is water in pipes. The battery is like a pump, voltage is like the water pressure it creates, current is like how much water flows each second, and resistance is like a narrow section of pipe that slows the flow.

The analogy has limits. Cut a water pipe and water pours out; cut a wire and the current just stops, because no charge spills out. And the battery doesn't fill empty wires: the charges are already in the wire, and it pushes them along. These are SI units, used everywhere; there's no US customary version of a volt or an amp.

Closed, open and short circuits

Current only flows around a complete loop. A closed circuit is an unbroken path from one battery terminal, through a device such as a bulb, and back to the other terminal. Break the loop anywhere and you have an open circuit: no current, nothing happens. A switch is just a neat way of opening and closing a circuit.

What the path is made of matters. Conductors, like copper, aluminum and other metals, let charge flow easily. Insulators, like plastic, rubber, glass and dry wood, don't. That's why wires are metal inside and wrapped in plastic.

A short circuit happens when a path with almost no resistance connects the battery's terminals directly, skipping the device. With almost nothing to limit it, the current gets very large. Wires and the battery heat up fast; they can burn you, melt insulation, and some batteries can leak, swell or catch fire. Never connect a bare wire straight across a battery, and never carry loose batteries in a pocket with coins or keys.

Ohm's law and power

For many parts, especially resistors, voltage, current and resistance are linked by Ohm's law:

V = I × R (voltage in volts = current in amps × resistance in ohms)

Rearrange it to find whichever one you're missing:

  • I = V ÷ R: a 1.5 V cell across 30 Ω gives 1.5 ÷ 30 = 0.05 A, or 50 mA.
  • R = V ÷ I: 9 V pushing 0.03 A means 9 ÷ 0.03 = 300 Ω.
  • V = I × R: 0.01 A through 470 Ω needs 0.01 × 470 = 4.7 V.

Use amps, not milliamps, in the formula, and convert at the end. Power is how fast energy is used, measured in watts (W): P = V × I. Power in a resistor turns into heat, so every resistor has a power rating it shouldn't go over. Small resistors like the ones in classroom sets are often rated for 0.25 W.

Series and parallel circuits

In a series circuit, parts are connected one after another in a single loop. The same current flows through each part, and resistances add: R = R1 + R2. If one part breaks, the loop opens and everything stops.

In a parallel circuit, each part gets its own branch between the same two points. Every branch has the same voltage across it, and the branch currents add up to the total. Because charge has more than one path, the total (equivalent) resistance is lower than any single branch. For two resistors in parallel:

R = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2)

Two equal resistors in parallel give exactly half of one. The lights in a house are wired in parallel, which is why switching off one lamp doesn't turn off the rest.

Worked example

Kai connects a 9 V battery to a single 450 Ω resistor. Using I = V ÷ R: 9 ÷ 450 = 0.02 A, which is 20 mA. The power is P = V × I = 9 × 0.02 = 0.18 W, under a typical 0.25 W rating.

Common mistake

Connecting an LED straight to a battery. An LED doesn't behave like a resistor: above its turn-on voltage, a small increase in voltage causes a big jump in current, and nothing inside the LED stops it. It overheats and can burn out in moments. Always put a current-limiting resistor in series with it.

Example only (real LEDs vary; check the part's data sheet): suppose an LED needs about 2 V and works well at 20 mA. From a 9 V battery, the resistor must take the other 9 − 2 = 7 V, so R = V ÷ I = 7 ÷ 0.020 = 350 Ω. If you don't have that exact value, use the next larger one you have, which gives slightly less current.

Key idea

Current needs a closed loop, and V = I × R links voltage, current and resistance. Series resistances add; parallel branches share the same voltage and lower the total. Always keep some resistance in the loop.

Code, data and testing

Code is just instructions. The hard part isn't typing them; it's making sure they do exactly what you meant, every time, including the cases you didn't think of.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Write simple pseudocode using sequence, decisions and loops.
  • Explain how sensors and microcontrollers turn inputs into outputs, and summarize readings with an average.
  • Plan tests with edge cases, compare expected and actual results, and debug one change at a time.

Why nearly every engineer writes code

You don't have to become a software engineer to use code. Today almost every engineering field relies on it in three big ways:

  • Controlling machines. Thermostats, traffic lights, washing machines, factory robots and car brakes all run small programs that decide what to do next.
  • Analyzing data. A civil engineer might study readings from sensors on a bridge; a biomedical engineer might look at heart-rate data from a medical device. Code sorts through thousands of numbers far faster than a person can.
  • Simulating designs. Engineers test a bridge, an airplane wing or a circuit on a computer before building it, so they can try many ideas cheaply and safely.

Whatever field you choose from Lesson 2, a little coding will make you a stronger engineer.

Algorithms and pseudocode

An algorithm is a set of step-by-step instructions for solving a problem. A recipe is an algorithm. A computer follows its instructions exactly, with no common sense to fill in gaps, so every step has to be clear.

Almost every algorithm is built from three structures:

  • Sequence: do steps in order, one after another.
  • Decisions: if something is true, do one thing; otherwise, do something else.
  • Loops: repeat steps, either a set number of times or until something changes.

Before writing real code, engineers often write pseudocode: plain-language steps that look a bit like code but don't follow any programming language's rules. Here's pseudocode for a fan that cools a small greenhouse:

  1. Repeat forever:
    1. Read the temperature.
    2. If temperature ≥ 28 °C, turn the fan on.
    3. Otherwise, turn the fan off.
    4. Wait 1 minute.

It uses all three structures. Once the logic is right, turning it into real code, in a free block-based coding tool or a text language, is much easier.

Sensors, microcontrollers and data

Many engineering projects follow the same pattern: input → processing → output.

  • Input: a sensor measures something in the world, like temperature, light, distance or moisture, and turns it into an electrical signal.
  • Processing: a microcontroller, a tiny computer on a small board, runs your program and decides what to do with that reading.
  • Output: something happens, such as a light turning on, a motor spinning or a number appearing on a screen.

Sensors also collect data. Single readings are noisy, so engineers usually take several and find the average (mean): add them up and divide by how many there are. Greenhouse readings of 21, 23, 22, 24 and 20 °C add up to 110, and 110 ÷ 5 = 22 °C.

Watch for readings that don't fit. If the sensor sat in direct sun for a moment and read 55 °C, the average of all six readings jumps to 165 ÷ 6 = 27.5 °C, even though the room never got that warm. Look for patterns, too: a graph with time along the bottom and the reading up the side makes trends easy to spot, like soil slowly drying over a week or a sudden spike that points to a faulty reading.

Testing: expected vs. actual

Testing code is the "test" step of the design process. You write test cases: specific inputs, each with the expected result you work out before running anything. Then you run the program and record the actual result. If they match, the test passes. If not, you've found a bug, and now you can fix it.

Don't just test typical values. Bugs love to hide at the boundaries, so include edge cases: values exactly at a limit, just above and just below it, and the smallest and largest values the sensor can give.

Worked example

Brooke is building an automatic plant waterer with a moisture sensor, a small microcontroller board and a small battery-powered pump. Her sensor gives a reading from 0 (bone dry) to 100 (soaking wet). Her plant needs water when the reading is 30 or lower. Her first pseudocode:

  1. Repeat forever:
    1. Read the soil moisture.
    2. If moisture < 30, turn the pump on for 5 seconds, then turn it off.
    3. Wait 30 minutes, so the water soaks in before the next reading.

Debugging, iteration and good records

Debugging is finding and fixing bugs, and it works best as a calm, step-by-step process:

  1. Reproduce the problem, so you can make it happen on purpose.
  2. Isolate it: narrow down which part of the program or which input causes it.
  3. Fix one thing at a time, so you know which change made the difference.
  4. Retest, including the tests that already passed, to be sure the fix didn't break something else.

This is iteration, the same loop as the design process from Lesson 1: define the problem, plan, build, test, improve, and repeat. Code is rarely right the first time, even for professionals.

Version history means saving numbered versions with a short note on what changed, so you can go back if a change makes things worse; many coding tools do this for you. Documentation means comments in your code and notes that explain what it does and why, so a teammate, or you in three months, can understand it.

Common mistake

Assuming code works because it ran without an error message. Brooke's first version ran perfectly smoothly; it just gave the wrong answer at 30. A program can run without crashing and still be wrong. Only comparing actual results against expected ones tells you it's correct.

Key idea

Plan your logic in pseudocode, decide the expected results before you test, include the edge cases, and fix one thing at a time.

Lesson 1 of 6 · about 15 minutes

What aerospace engineers do

Every paper airplane you've ever folded was a small aerospace engineering project: a design, a test flight and a tweak.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain the difference between aeronautics and astronautics.
  • Name the main subfields of aerospace engineering and what each one worries about.
  • Describe realistic paths into the field, starting in high school.

Two halves of one field

Aerospace engineering is the design, building and testing of things that fly. It splits into two overlapping halves.

Aeronautics is about vehicles that fly inside the atmosphere and use the air to stay up: airplanes, helicopters, gliders and drones. Their wings or rotors push on the air, and the air pushes back.

Astronautics is about vehicles that travel beyond most of the atmosphere, into space: rockets, satellites and spacecraft. Up there, there's almost no air to push on, so the rules change, and you'll see how in Lessons 5 and 6.

Many engineers work across both. A rocket spends its first minutes flying through thick air, so it has to be good at aeronautics before it ever gets to astronautics.

What aerospace engineers work on

  • Aircraft: passenger jets, small propeller planes, helicopters, gliders and the airports and air traffic systems they depend on.
  • Rockets: vehicles that carry satellites, science instruments and people off the ground.
  • Satellites: spacecraft that circle the Earth for weather forecasting, communication, navigation and science.
  • Drones: aircraft with no pilot on board, from tiny quadcopters to large airplanes used for mapping, farming and search and rescue.

Aerospace engineers also work on the tools behind these vehicles: wind tunnels, computer simulations, test stands and the software that flies them.

Common mistake

Thinking aerospace engineers are pilots or astronauts. Most aerospace engineers never fly the vehicles they design. They spend their days calculating, drawing, building prototypes, running tests and writing reports. Some pilots and astronauts do have engineering degrees, but flying and designing are different jobs.

The five main subfields

No one person designs a whole airplane or rocket. The work is split into specialties that have to fit together:

  • Aerodynamics: how air flows around the vehicle. How much lift do the wings make? How much drag slows it down?
  • Propulsion: what pushes the vehicle forward or upward: propellers, jet engines, rocket motors, and the fuel or batteries that power them.
  • Structures: the frame and skin. Is it strong and stiff enough to survive flight loads, while staying as light as possible?
  • Controls: how the vehicle is steered and kept steady, including the sensors, computers and moving surfaces that do it.
  • Systems: making all the parts work together, tracking the overall weight, power and cost, and making sure the finished vehicle does what it was meant to do.

These pull against each other. Structures wants thicker parts for strength; propulsion and aerodynamics want less weight. Every aerospace design is full of trade-offs, just like the ones you met in Engineering Foundations.

Aerospace and the design process

Aerospace engineers use the same engineering design process you learned in Engineering Foundations: define the problem, brainstorm, pick a design, prototype, test and improve. Two things make aerospace a little different.

First, weight matters enormously. Every extra gram has to be lifted, so engineers fight over grams the way Riley's club does.

Second, failure in flight is dangerous, so testing is careful and step by step. Engineers use computer simulations, then wind tunnels and ground tests, and only then real flights, starting small. It's the same reason you test a paper glider with gentle throws before a hard one.

The four forces of flight

An airplane cruising at a steady speed has every force on it perfectly balanced, which means, in a sense, it's doing exactly what a book lying on a table does.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Name the four forces of flight and the direction each one acts.
  • Explain which forces balance in steady level flight, and what changes when an aircraft climbs, descends or speeds up.
  • Measure a glider's glide ratio and descent angle.

Four forces, four directions

Every aircraft in flight has four forces acting on it. You met forces and the newton in Engineering Foundations; here they come in two opposing pairs.

  • Weight pulls the aircraft down toward the Earth. It equals mass × g, where g = 9.81 m/s². A 1 kg model weighs 9.81 N.
  • Lift pushes up, made by the wings as they move through the air. It works against weight.
  • Thrust pushes the aircraft forward, from a propeller, a jet engine or, in a paper airplane, your throw.
  • Drag is the air resisting the motion, pulling backward. It works against thrust.

Strictly, lift acts at right angles to the direction of flight and drag acts straight back along it. In level flight that means lift is straight up and drag is straight back, which is how you'll usually see them drawn.

Balanced forces: steady level flight

Newton's first law says that when the forces on an object balance, it keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction. That includes standing still, but it also includes cruising at a steady 200 km/h.

So in steady level flight, meaning constant speed and constant height, the forces are balanced:

  • Lift = weight, so the aircraft neither rises nor sinks.
  • Thrust = drag, so it neither speeds up nor slows down.

A 1,200 kg airplane in steady level flight weighs 1,200 × 9.81 = 11,772 N, so its wings must be making 11,772 N of lift, no more and no less.

Common mistake

Thinking thrust must be bigger than drag just to keep an airplane moving. If thrust were bigger than drag, the airplane would keep speeding up. To cruise at a steady speed, thrust only has to equal drag. An unbalanced force is needed to change speed or direction, not to keep going.

Speeding up, climbing and descending

To change what the aircraft is doing, the pilot unbalances the forces for a while.

  • Speeding up: more thrust than drag makes the aircraft accelerate. Drag grows as speed rises, so eventually drag catches up with the new thrust and the aircraft settles at a faster steady speed.
  • Climbing: the aircraft needs extra thrust, because it's now lifting its weight higher as well as pushing through the air. In a steady climb, thrust has to cover drag and part of the weight, since the aircraft is pointed partly upward. Surprisingly, lift doesn't need to be bigger than weight during a steady climb; the extra comes from thrust.
  • Descending: the pilot reduces thrust and points the nose slightly down. Now part of the weight acts along the flight path and helps pull the aircraft forward, so it can keep its speed with less thrust while it loses height.

Gliders: trading height for distance

A glider has no engine, so it has no thrust at all. How does it keep moving forward against drag? It flies on a path that slopes gently downward. Tilted like that, part of its weight acts along the path and pulls it forward, doing the job thrust would do. The price is height: a glider in still air is always coming down.

Engineers describe how well it does this with the glide ratio: the distance it travels along the ground divided by the height it loses. A glider with a glide ratio of 6 : 1 travels 6 m forward for every 1 m it drops. The descent angle is the angle between its flight path and the ground; a better glider has a higher ratio and a shallower angle. High-performance sailplanes can manage more than 40 : 1.

In a steady glide in still air, the glide ratio equals the glider's lift divided by its drag. That's why engineers designing a good glider focus on keeping drag low. Try the calculator below with your own glider test.

Wings and lift

A wing holds up an airplane by throwing a huge amount of air toward the ground, and it does it without a single moving part.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Describe an airfoil and explain how angle of attack changes lift.
  • Explain lift correctly, in terms of turning air downward and lower pressure above the wing, and spot the equal-transit-time myth.
  • Use the lift equation, and explain what a stall is and what flaps do.

The shape of a wing

Slice through a wing from front to back and the cut end's shape is called an airfoil. Most airfoils have a rounded front, the leading edge, and a thin, sharp back, the trailing edge.

Draw a straight line from the leading edge to the trailing edge: that's the chord line. Many airfoils are curved, with the top bulging more than the bottom. That curve is called camber, and it helps the wing make lift even when it meets the air almost head-on.

Camber helps, but it isn't required. A flat sheet of paper tilted into the air makes lift too, which is why a paper airplane with flat wings still flies.

Angle of attack

The angle of attack is the angle between the wing's chord line and the air flowing toward it. It isn't the same as where the nose is pointing compared with the ground: a glider can be pointing slightly downhill and still have its wing meeting the oncoming air at a positive angle.

Tilt the wing up a little more, and it makes more lift; tilt it down, and it makes less. That's why an airplane flying slowly holds its nose higher than one flying fast: at lower speed it needs a bigger angle to make the same lift.

How a wing actually makes lift

As a wing moves through the air, it turns the air downward. Air flowing over the top follows the curved, tilted surface and leaves the trailing edge heading down, and air hitting the underside is deflected down too. This downward-moving air is called downwash. By Newton's third law, if the wing pushes air down, the air pushes the wing up.

You can describe the same flow using pressure. The pressure above the wing is lower than the pressure below it, so the air underneath pushes up harder than the air on top pushes down. The difference, spread over the whole wing, is the lift. The air over the top also moves faster than the air underneath. Turning air down and lower pressure on top aren't competing theories; they describe the same flow.

You may have heard a different explanation: the air splitting at the front must "meet up again" at the back, so the air over the longer top surface has to go faster. This equal transit time idea is a common myth. Nothing forces the two streams to meet, and in reality the air over the top reaches the trailing edge well before the air underneath. The myth also can't explain flat paper wings, or airplanes flying upside down.

The lift equation

Engineers predict lift with this equation:

L = ½ ρ v² S CL

In words: lift equals one half, times air density, times airspeed squared, times wing area, times the lift coefficient.

  • L is lift, in newtons.
  • ρ (the Greek letter rho) is air density. At sea level it's about 1.225 kg/m³. Higher up, air is thinner: Colorado Springs sits about 1,840 m above sea level, where standard air density is about 1.02 kg/m³, and less on a hot day.
  • v is airspeed, in m/s: the speed of the wing through the air.
  • S is wing area, in m², seen from above.
  • CL is the lift coefficient, a number with no units that captures the airfoil's shape and angle of attack. Bigger angle of attack means bigger CL, up to the stall.

Notice that speed is squared. Double the airspeed and lift goes up four times. Try the calculator below with your own numbers.

Stall and flaps

Lift increases with angle of attack, but only up to a point. Past the critical angle of attack, often somewhere around 15° for many wings, the air can no longer follow the curved top surface. It breaks away into swirling flow, lift drops sharply and drag jumps. That's a stall. The fix is to lower the nose so the wing meets the air at a smaller angle.

A stall is set by angle of attack, not speed. It's most likely when flying slowly, because slow flight needs a big angle of attack, but a wing can stall at any speed if the angle gets too large. A paper glider that swoops up, slows, and then drops is stalling.

Flaps are hinged panels on the trailing edge of the wing that swing down (and on some aircraft slide back). They add camber, and often area, raising CL, so the aircraft can make enough lift at the slow speeds used for takeoff and landing. They add drag too, which helps an airplane come down on a steeper path when landing.

Common mistake

Thinking a stall means the engine stopped. In aerospace, a stall is about the wing, not the engine: the angle of attack got too large and the air stopped flowing smoothly over the top. A glider has no engine at all, and it can still stall.

Key idea

A wing makes lift by turning air downward, which is the same as having lower pressure above it than below. Lift grows with air density, wing area, lift coefficient and the square of speed, until the angle of attack gets too big and the wing stalls.

Stability and control

Making a wing that lifts is only half the job. The other half is making the aircraft fly where you point it, and keep flying that way when the air gives it a shove.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Explain why a glider's center of gravity has to sit toward the nose.
  • Name pitch, roll and yaw and the control surface that handles each one.
  • Trim a paper or foam glider by making small changes, one at a time.

Two special points: center of gravity and center of pressure

Every aircraft has a center of gravity (CG): the single point where all of its weight effectively acts. Balance a glider on one fingertip, and the spot where it sits level is its CG. Weight added near the nose moves the CG forward.

The air pushes on the wing and tail too. The center of pressure is the point where all that push effectively acts. Where these two points sit relative to each other decides whether an aircraft flies steadily or tumbles.

Remember moments from Engineering Foundations: a force acting at a distance from a pivot. An aircraft in flight turns about its CG, so any force that doesn't act through the CG tips the nose up or down.

Why a glider needs its weight toward the nose

An aircraft is stable when, after something disturbs it (a gust, a wobbly launch), it tends to return to how it was flying on its own. For a glider, that means the CG must sit ahead of the center of pressure.

Think of a dart: heavy at the front, light fins at the back. If it points off course, the air pushes the fins back into line. A glider's tail works the same way. With the CG well forward, a nose-up bump makes the tail meet the air at a steeper angle, and the tail's extra lift pushes the nose back down.

Throw a dart fins first and it flips around. A glider with its CG too far back behaves the same way: small disturbances grow instead of dying out. That's why foam gliders often carry a bit of clay or a clip up front.

Too much nose weight is a problem too: a very nose-heavy glider is stable but dives steeply and lands short.

Pitch, roll and yaw

An aircraft can rotate in three ways, each around an imaginary line (an axis) through its CG:

  • Pitch is the nose moving up or down, rotating around a line running from wingtip to wingtip.
  • Roll is one wing dipping while the other rises, rotating around a line running from nose to tail.
  • Yaw is the nose swinging left or right, rotating around a line running straight up and down.

To remember them: nod "yes" for pitch, tilt your head toward a shoulder for roll, and shake "no" for yaw.

Control surfaces

A control surface is a hinged part of the wing or tail. Moving one changes the airflow and creates a small force far from the CG, and that moment rotates the aircraft.

  • The elevator, on the back edge of the horizontal tail, controls pitch. Raise it and the air pushes the tail down, so the nose comes up. Lower it and the nose goes down.
  • The ailerons, near the tips of the wings, control roll. They move in opposite directions: the aileron that goes up reduces lift on that wing, and the one that goes down increases lift on the other, so the aircraft rolls toward the raised aileron.
  • The rudder, on the back edge of the vertical tail fin, controls yaw. Bend it to the right and the air pushes the tail left, so the nose swings right.

A paper or foam glider has no hinges, so tiny bends in the back edge of the wing or tail act as fixed control surfaces.

Trimming: small changes, one at a time

Trim means making small, lasting adjustments so an aircraft flies steadily with no one touching the controls. For a glider, here's what to try:

  • Stalls (climbs, slows, drops): add a little nose weight, or bend the elevator's back edge down a tiny amount.
  • Dives: remove a little nose weight, or bend the elevator's back edge up a tiny amount.
  • Turns when you want it straight: bend the rudder's back edge a little the opposite way, or check that both wings are the same shape and not twisted.

Make each bend smaller than you think you need: a millimeter or two makes a real difference on a small glider. Change one thing at a time and fly two or three times after each change. If you add weight and bend the tail at once, you can't tell which one helped. It's the design process's test-and-improve loop on a small scale.

Launch with a gentle, level throw, and always throw away from people, especially their faces.

Common mistake

Fixing a stall by throwing harder. A faster throw makes a tail-heavy glider climb even more steeply before it stalls. The problem is balance, not speed: move the CG forward first, then use a gentle throw to judge the result.

Key idea

Keep the center of gravity ahead of the center of pressure so the aircraft corrects itself, then use the elevator, ailerons and rudder, or small bends, to control pitch, roll and yaw.

Rockets and propulsion

A rocket doesn't push against the ground or the air. It throws mass out the back as fast as it can, and that is enough to reach space.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Use Newton's third law to explain how rockets and jet engines make thrust.
  • Calculate a rocket's thrust-to-weight ratio and decide whether it will lift off.
  • Explain why big rockets use stages, and follow the basic rules of model rocketry safety.

Push something back, get pushed forward

Newton's third law says that when one object pushes on another, the second object pushes back on the first with a force of the same size in the opposite direction. Forces always come in pairs.

You've felt this. Step off a skateboard and the board shoots backward. Let go of a blown-up balloon and it zips around the room as the air rushes out. The balloon pushes the air out of its neck, and the air pushes the balloon the other way.

Every engine that flies works on this idea. It pushes a stream of gas, called the exhaust, out the back, and the exhaust pushes the engine forward. That forward push is thrust, which you met in Lesson 2. The more mass the engine throws out each second, and the faster it throws it, the more thrust it makes.

This also clears up a common confusion: a rocket doesn't need air or ground to push against. The push is between the rocket and its own exhaust, which is why rockets work in the empty space above the atmosphere.

Jet engines vs. rockets

Fuel needs oxygen to burn. Where that oxygen comes from is the big difference between the two main kinds of engine.

  • A jet engine breathes. It pulls air in at the front, squeezes it, burns fuel using the oxygen in that air, and blasts the hot gas out the back. It carries only fuel, which keeps it light, but it can't work where there isn't enough air.
  • A rocket engine carries everything it needs: the fuel and an oxidizer, a substance that supplies the oxygen for burning. Together these are called propellant. Because it brings its own oxidizer, a rocket works in space.

Some rockets use liquid propellants, such as liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen stored in separate tanks and pumped into the engine. Others use solid propellant, with the fuel and oxidizer already mixed into a solid block. The small motors used in model rockets are solid-propellant motors.

Thrust-to-weight ratio: will it lift off?

A rocket sitting on the pad has two main forces on it: its weight pulling down, and its thrust pushing up. Weight is mass × g, where g = 9.81 m/s². The thrust-to-weight ratio compares them:

Thrust-to-weight ratio = thrust ÷ weight

If the ratio is less than 1, or exactly 1, the rocket sits there no matter how loud it is. It lifts off only when thrust is greater than weight. The leftover force, thrust − weight, is the net upward force, and dividing it by the mass gives the starting acceleration (Newton's second law, a = F ÷ m).

Airplanes are different. An airliner's engines make much less thrust than its weight, because the wings do the lifting; the engines only need to beat drag. A rocket going straight up has no wings to help, so its thrust has to do it all.

Worked example

Andre is building a model rocket from a kit. Fully loaded with its motor, it has a mass of 0.15 kg. The motor the kit recommends has an average thrust of 10 N.

  • Weight = 0.15 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 1.4715 N ≈ 1.5 N
  • Thrust-to-weight ratio = 10 ÷ 1.4715 ≈ 6.80
  • Net upward force = 10 − 1.4715 ≈ 8.5 N
  • Starting acceleration = 8.5285 N (unrounded) ÷ 0.15 kg ≈ 56.86 m/s²

That's about 5.8 times g, which is one less than the thrust-to-weight ratio (one g's worth of thrust is spent just holding the rocket up). Andre then wonders what would happen with a heavier rocket and the same motor:

Staging: drop what you don't need

Most of a big rocket's mass at liftoff is propellant. As it burns, the tanks empty, but the rocket is still carrying those big, heavy, empty tanks and the engines that emptied them. That's dead weight, and pushing it costs propellant.

Staging solves this. The rocket is built as two or more rockets stacked together. The bottom stage fires first; when its propellant runs out, it separates and falls away, and the next stage's engine lights. Each stage only has to push a lighter and lighter rocket, which is why most rockets that reach orbit use two or more stages.

Common mistake

Thinking a rocket needs something to push against, so it couldn't work in space. The exhaust is what it pushes against. In fact, a rocket engine works slightly better in a vacuum, because there's no outside air pressure pushing back on the exhaust.

Model rocketry safety

Model rocketry is a great way to see all of this for real, as long as you do it safely:

  • Use only commercially made, certified motors and commercially made kits, and only the motors your kit lists.
  • Never make, mix or modify propellants or motors, and never take a motor apart. Homemade propellants cause serious injuries.
  • Fly with an adult, and follow a national model rocketry safety code. It covers launch distances, launch controllers with a safety key, field size, weather and what to do if a motor doesn't ignite.
  • Launch only in a large open area away from buildings, trees, power lines and dry grass, and never point a rocket at a person or anything that could be harmed.

Treat the safety code as part of the engineering, not an extra. Real launch teams follow checklists for the same reason.

Reaching space and orbit

Astronauts on the space station aren't floating because gravity switched off. They're falling the whole time, and moving sideways so fast that they keep missing the Earth.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to

  • Describe where space is usually said to begin, and why it's a line people chose.
  • Explain an orbit as falling around the Earth while moving sideways fast enough.
  • Give examples of what satellites do and describe paths into aerospace careers.

Where does space begin?

The atmosphere doesn't stop at a sharp edge. It just gets thinner and thinner the higher you go. So "where space begins" is a boundary people agree on, not a wall you could touch.

The most commonly used boundary is 100 km (about 62 miles) above sea level, called the Kármán line after the engineer and scientist Theodore von Kármán. The idea behind it: up there, the air is so thin that a wing would have to move at nearly orbital speed to make enough lift to hold an aircraft up. Around this height, aeronautics (flying with wings) gives way to astronautics (flying in space). Some organizations draw the line a little lower, but 100 km is the one you'll see most often.

Reaching 100 km isn't the hard part: a rocket can go straight up past it and fall straight back down. Staying in space is mostly about going sideways.

An orbit is falling and missing

Picture standing on a very tall mountain, above the air, and throwing a ball sideways. Gravity pulls it down, and it curves to the ground some distance away. Throw it harder and it lands farther away. Now throw it so hard that, as it falls, the Earth's curved surface drops away beneath it just as fast as it falls. The ball keeps falling but never reaches the ground. That is an orbit.

So a satellite isn't floating above gravity. Gravity is exactly what keeps pulling it around the curve. Without gravity, it would fly off in a straight line; without enough sideways speed, it would fall back into the atmosphere.

For the lowest practical orbits, a few hundred kilometers up, that sideways speed, called orbital speed, is roughly 7.8 km/s. That's about 28,000 km/h, or about 17,500 mph. At that speed you'd cover the 100 km up to the edge of space in under 13 seconds. This is why most of a rocket's work goes into speeding up sideways, not just climbing: after launching straight up, it gradually tips over until it's flying nearly parallel to the ground.

Worked example

Lena wants to check that "about 8 km/s" really is the right speed, using only numbers she can reason about. She ignores air and mountains and walks through it step by step:

  1. How far does something fall in one second? Starting from rest, the distance fallen is ½ × g × t² = 0.5 × 9.81 × 1² ≈ 4.9 m.
  2. How fast does the Earth curve away? Earth's radius is about 6,371 km. Over a straight line 8 km long, the surface curves down below that line by about 8,000² ÷ (2 × 6,371,000) ≈ 5.0 m.
  3. Put them together. An object moving sideways at about 8 km/s travels 8 km in one second. In that second it falls about 5 m, and the ground also curves away by about 5 m. It has fallen, but it's no closer to the ground.
  4. Check the real numbers. Gravity gets a little weaker with height, so orbits higher up need slightly less speed:

Satellites and the space station

A satellite is any object that orbits a larger one. The Moon is a natural satellite of the Earth; the ones engineers build are artificial satellites. They do jobs you rely on every day:

  • Navigation: signals from several satellites let your phone work out where you are.
  • Weather: satellites watch clouds and storms form and track hurricanes.
  • Communication: they relay TV, phone and internet signals across oceans and to remote places.
  • Earth observation: they map crops, wildfires, floods and sea ice.
  • Science: space telescopes see without the atmosphere blurring the view.

Some satellites use a geostationary orbit, about 35,800 km above the equator. At that height, one trip around takes the same time as one turn of the Earth, so the satellite seems to hang over one spot in the sky. That's why a satellite TV dish can be bolted in place.

The International Space Station, built and run by NASA and partner space agencies from several countries, orbits about 400 km up. Crews live aboard for months and run experiments. Because it circles the Earth about every 92 minutes, the crew sees roughly 15 or 16 sunrises and sunsets every day.

Inside, everything floats. But at 400 km, gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they and the station are falling around the Earth together, so nothing presses them against the floor. This is called microgravity.

Common mistake

Saying there's no gravity in space. If there were no gravity at 400 km, the space station would fly off in a straight line instead of circling the Earth. Astronauts feel weightless because they're in free fall, not because gravity is missing.

Careers, and a look back

Space work needs far more than astronauts. Aerospace engineers design satellites and launch vehicles, plan orbits, build the structures and heat shields that survive launch and return, write flight software, and run tests on the ground. Technicians assemble and test the hardware, and people from mechanical, electrical and computer engineering, physics and math all work on space projects. NASA (NASA.gov) has free student resources and lists internship programs, and the FAA (FAA.gov) covers careers on the aviation side.
